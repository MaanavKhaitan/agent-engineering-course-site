Module 08 — Evaluation & Testing

You have built an agent. Scout reads files, runs bash, searches, retrieves from a knowledge base, manages its own context. It works in the demo. Now your manager asks the question every engineer eventually has to answer about every system they ship: how do you know it works? — and, the harder follow-up, how will you know when it stops working?

For a deterministic service you already have the answer in your bones. You write unit tests. Same input, same output, green check, merge. CI runs them on every commit; a regression turns the build red before it reaches a user. This discipline is so deeply wired into how senior engineers think that it feels less like a practice and more like gravity.

This module is about the uncomfortable discovery that gravity does not work here. An agent is a stochastic system sitting on top of a dependency — the model — that can change behavior underneath you without a single line of your code changing. The testing instincts that have served you for a decade do not transfer cleanly, and the places where they break are specific and worth naming precisely. So we are going to do two things. First, take your testing intuition apart and show exactly which struts snap under nondeterminism. Then rebuild — not from unit testing, but from benchmarking and statistics, which is the tradition the field actually borrowed from. By the end you will have built a real, reusable eval harness for Scout, used it to make a genuine ship/no-ship decision, and calibrated the judge that grades it.

This is the most important module in the course. Not because evals are glamorous — they are the opposite of glamorous, which is precisely why undisciplined teams skip them and ship agents that fail silently in production. The teams that ship reliable agents treat eval engineering as first-class work, comparable in effort to building the product itself. By the end of this module you will understand why.

Learning objectives

By the end of this module you will be able to:

  • Explain precisely why you cannot unit-test an agent, in terms of four failures: nondeterminism, the space of acceptable answers, continuous quality, and dependency drift (“prompt rot”).
  • Distinguish an eval (your private, bespoke measurement) from a benchmark (a shared, standardized eval for comparing models), and know which one answers which question.
  • Decompose any eval into its three parts — dataset, grader, aggregation — and choose well within each.
  • Pick the right grader: programmatic, LLM-as-judge, or human — and know the trust ordering and failure mode of each.
  • Aggregate stochastic runs into rates with confidence intervals, and distinguish pass@k from pass^k.
  • Run a real eval operationally: isolated fixtures, mocked side effects, parallelism, smoke-vs-full tiering, full transcript capture.
  • Use evals to make decisions: regression gating in CI, model selection, drift detection — and validate the grader itself.
  • Build the production flywheel where every escalation becomes a permanent regression case.

A note on scope. Evals consume traces — the full transcript of an agent run — and producing those traces well is Module 09’s job. Here we treat the trace as the raw material of a grade; there we make it observable. The evaluator-optimizer loop from Module 06 is the same machinery pointed inward at generation time rather than at CI; we connect them but don’t re-teach orchestration.

1. Why your testing intuition breaks

Let’s be specific about what a unit test assumes, because every assumption is about to fail.

A unit test asserts that f(x) == expected. For this to be a useful, stable test, four things must hold: f is deterministic (same x, same output); there is a single correct expected; correctness is binary (equal or not); and f’s dependencies are pinned (nothing changes underneath without a version bump you control). For an agent built on an LLM, all four fail at once.

Nondeterminism: same input, different output. Call the model twice with identical inputs and you get two different completions. Sampling is probabilistic; even at temperature=0 you are not guaranteed bit-identical output across requests, because batching, hardware, and kernel nondeterminism leak through. So assert agent(task) == expected is not occasionally flaky — it is categorically the wrong shape. There is no single output to assert against. Your CI mental model — green is green, red is red, a flake is a bug to be fixed — assumes determinism that does not exist here. A test that passes 80% of the time isn’t a broken test; it’s an accurate measurement of an 80%-reliable behavior, and the information is in the 80%, not in the pass/fail of any single run.

No single correct answer: a space, not a point. Ask Scout to “summarize this incident report” and there is no canonical string. There is a region of acceptable summaries — many phrasings, lengths, and emphases that a competent human would call correct — surrounded by a region of unacceptable ones (missed the root cause, hallucinated a service name, three paragraphs of fluff). Equality is meaningless. The question is never “does the output equal X” but “does the output land inside the acceptable region,” and that region is defined by properties, not by a golden string.

Continuous quality: better and worse, not pass and fail. Two summaries can both be acceptable while one is clearly better. Quality is a scalar — frequently a fuzzy, human-judgment scalar — not a boolean. A prompt change might move average quality from 7.2 to 7.6 on some rubric. There is no unit-test vocabulary for “got somewhat better.” Your suite needs to express distributions over a quality scale, and to tell a real 0.4 improvement from noise.

Dependency drift — “prompt rot”: the one that should scare you most. In a normal service, dependencies are pinned. requests==2.31.0 behaves identically today and next year; an upgrade is an explicit, reviewable diff, and if it breaks an interface the compiler or type checker tells you at build time. The model is a dependency with none of these properties. The provider ships claude-sonnet-4-6 → claude-sonnet-4-7 and your behavior shifts — usually better, sometimes subtly worse on your specific prompts, which were implicitly tuned against quirks of the old version. Your code did not change. Your tests (if you wrote unit tests) still pass, because they assert on your code, not on model behavior. There is no compiler, no type system, no failing build to catch a model upgrade silently changing what your agent does. This is the deepest reason agents need evals: they are the only mechanism that turns “the model changed” from an invisible, existential risk into a visible, measured diff.

📖 The term prompt rot names the slow divergence between a prompt and the model it was tuned against. Your prompt is a contract co-evolved with a specific model’s behavior. Upgrade the model and the contract is silently renegotiated. Without an eval suite you find out from a user, in production, weeks later.

So unit testing is the wrong tool — not because testing is wrong but because its assumptions don’t hold. What does hold its assumptions? Benchmarking and statistics. You don’t ask “is this one output correct.” You run a population of tasks many times and score the results statistically — distributions, rates, confidence intervals. You stop thinking like someone asserting equality and start thinking like someone running an A/B test or a clinical trial: measuring a noisy effect against a baseline and asking whether the difference is real. That mental shift is the whole module. Everything below is mechanics in service of it.

✅ The reframe in one line. Unit testing asks “is this output correct?” Evaluation asks “across a representative population of tasks, run enough times, how often does my agent land in the acceptable region — and is that rate meaningfully different from my baseline?”

2. Eval vs benchmark

These words get used interchangeably and they should not be. The distinction determines which question you’re answering.

A benchmark is the standardized, shared, leaderboard kind of eval. SWE-bench, GPQA, MMLU, τ-bench. A fixed dataset, a fixed grader, published so everyone scores the same way. Its purpose is to compare models on a common axis — it’s how you read “the new model is 12 points better at agentic coding.” Benchmarks are public goods: they let the whole field talk about capability in shared units.

An eval is any measurement of system quality. Usually private and bespoke to your task — your cases, your grader, your definition of “good.” Its purpose is to answer “does my system do my job well enough to ship?”

The clean way to hold the relationship: all benchmarks are evals; most evals are not benchmarks. A benchmark is the special case of an eval that has been standardized and shared for cross-model comparison.

The two answer different questions and you need both:

  • Benchmarks → model selection. “Is Opus or Sonnet better at the kind of work Scout does?” Read public benchmarks for a first cut, then confirm on your own eval — see below for why you can’t stop at the benchmark.
  • Private evals → does my system work. No public benchmark measures whether Scout resolves your customers’ tickets. Only a private eval built from your tasks does. This is the one you actually gate ships on.

Three reasons you cannot outsource your judgment to public benchmarks:

  • Contamination. Benchmarks leak into training data. A model scoring well on SWE-bench may have seen SWE-bench tasks. The score conflates capability with memorization, and you can’t tell the ratio from outside.
  • Goodhart’s law. “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” Public benchmarks are targets the entire industry optimizes toward. Models get tuned to them, which inflates scores relative to the messy real tasks the benchmark was meant to proxy.
  • Construct-validity gap. A benchmark measures a proxy for what you care about. SWE-bench measures “resolves GitHub issues in these 12 Python repos.” That is genuinely informative and genuinely not your codebase, your stack, your bug distribution, your definition of an acceptable fix.

This is why your private eval suite is a strategic asset — a moat. Anyone can read the same public leaderboard. The thing competitors cannot copy is your curated set of your real tasks with your graders, accumulated from your production failures. It is the most durable artifact in your whole agent stack: models churn, frameworks churn, but a well-curated eval suite compounds in value every month you feed it. Treat it like production code, because it is.

3. The anatomy of an eval

Every eval, from a one-off script to a 5,000-case suite, has the same three parts. Get all three right or the number lies to you.

Dataset (what to test on) → Grader (how to score one run) → Aggregation (how to turn many noisy scores into a decision)

3a. The dataset — cases, but a “case” is a whole world

A case is one task you grade the agent on. For a prompt eval, a case is an input and a grading key. For an agent, a case is heavier: it is an entire environment / fixture — a directory of files, a seeded database, mocked tool responses, an initial state — because an agent acts on an environment, not just responds to a string. “Fix the failing test in this repo” only means something if “this repo” is a concrete, frozen tree of files.

Two properties make a dataset trustworthy:

  • Frozen and reproducible. A case must produce the same starting world every single time you run it, on any machine, today and in six months. If the fixture drifts — a file edited in place, a date that depends on now(), a network call to a live service — your eval measures the drift, not the agent. Freeze the fixture; copy it fresh for each trial (we do exactly this in the exercise).
  • Tagged for slicing. Each case carries metadata: category (bugfix / extraction / qa), difficulty, source, whatever cleaves your task space. You almost never want one aggregate number; you want per-slice numbers, because “82% overall” can hide “bugfix collapsed from 90% to 60% while extraction rose and masked it.” Tags are how you see the collapse.

Where cases come from — three sources, and the order matters:

  1. Hand-authored seeds. You sit down and write the obvious tasks the agent must handle. This bootstraps the suite and encodes your spec. It is also where every suite starts and where weak suites stop.
  2. Production-mined failures — the flywheel, and the most valuable source by far. Every time Scout fails in production — an escalation, a human correction, a thumbs-down, a reverted commit — you freeze that exact situation as a new case with the correct outcome as its grading key. Now it is a permanent regression test. The agent can never silently re-break it. This is the engine that makes an eval suite a moat: it grows from reality, automatically covering the failures that actually happen rather than the ones you imagined. A team with a disciplined flywheel pulls away from one without it every single week.
  3. Synthetic and adversarial. Use a model to generate cases — permute parameters, manufacture edge cases, write deliberately nasty inputs (injection attempts, contradictory instructions, empty files, enormous files). Cheap breadth, but lower trust: synthetic cases can be unrealistic or mislabeled, so they supplement seeds and mined cases rather than replace them.

📖 The flywheel reframes incidents. In a normal service a bug is fixed and forgotten. In an agent, every production failure is a free, perfectly-realistic eval case handed to you by reality — provided you have the discipline to capture it before the trace scrolls away. That discipline is the difference between an agent that gets more reliable over time and one that re-breaks the same way every quarter.

3b. The grader — three families, in descending order of trust

The grader scores one run. Pick the most trustworthy grader the task allows — and design tasks to allow the trustworthy ones.

1. Programmatic (code checks the outcome) — the gold standard. A deterministic function inspects the resulting world and returns pass/fail or a score. Did the test suite go green? Does the file contain the expected function? Is the emitted JSON valid against the schema? Did the SQL return the right rows? When you can write a programmatic grader, you almost always should: it is deterministic, free, fast, and cannot be fooled. The constraint is that not everything is mechanically checkable — but far more is than you’d think if you design the task to be checkable. “Fix the bug” is fuzzy; “fix the bug such that pytest passes” is programmatic. Engineering the task toward a programmatic grader is one of the highest-leverage moves in eval design.

A common, sharp sub-form is the assertion list — a set of must and must_not predicates over the output or final state. Must contain the ticket ID; must not contain a raw API key; must leave the test suite green. Cheap to write, composable, and surprisingly expressive for “did it do the obviously-required things and avoid the obviously-forbidden ones.”

2. LLM-as-judge (a model scores against a rubric) — for fuzzy quality at scale. When the criterion is genuinely subjective — “is this a good summary?” — you use a second model as the grader, prompted with an explicit rubric. This unlocks scale for criteria no assert can express. It is also the grader most likely to lie to you, so it comes with non-negotiable discipline:

  • Calibrate against humans. A judge is worthless until you’ve checked its verdicts against human labels on a sample and measured agreement. An uncalibrated judge is a random number generator wearing a lab coat. (You’ll do this in part (e) of the exercise.)
  • Know the biases. Judges exhibit length bias (longer = better, regardless of substance), self-style / self-enhancement bias (prefers outputs that read like the judge’s own model would write), and position bias (in pairwise comparison, favors whichever answer came first — so swap order and average). These are documented, reproducible, and they make a judge confidently wrong.
  • Force it to cite evidence. Make the judge quote the specific spans that justify its verdict before it scores. This both improves accuracy and gives you something to audit — a verdict with no evidence is unfalsifiable.
  • Use structured output. Constrain the verdict to a typed schema (Pydantic + messages.parse) so you get a score, a reason, and cited evidence — never free-form prose you have to regex. We do exactly this below.
  • Prefer a strong judge. Use a frontier model (claude-opus-4-8) as judge even when the system under test is cheaper (Scout on Sonnet). Judging is often harder than doing, and a weak judge is the dominant source of eval noise.

3. Human (a person scores) — ground truth, used sparingly. The highest-trust grader and the least scalable. Reserve humans for two jobs: establishing the ground-truth labels you calibrate the judge against, and spot-reading transcripts to catch failures your automated graders miss (especially passing-for-the-wrong-reason). You will not human-grade your whole suite on every run; you human-grade a sample to keep the cheap graders honest.

✅ The grader heuristic. Reach for programmatic first and engineer the task to allow it. Use an LLM judge only for genuinely fuzzy quality, and only after calibrating it against humans. Keep humans in the loop as the calibration anchor, not the bulk grader.

3c. Aggregation — from noisy runs to a decision

This is where the statistics live, and where most homegrown evals quietly go wrong. Because the system is stochastic, one run of one case tells you almost nothing. You run each case k times and report rates with confidence intervals — never a single boolean.

Two metrics, and the difference between them is the difference between a demo and an autonomous product:

  • pass@k — “at least 1 of k runs succeeded.” Optimistic. Relevant when a human or a retry can salvage a failure: if the agent gets it right one try in three and someone picks the good one, pass@k is the metric you care about.
  • pass^k — “all k runs succeeded.” The reliability metric. This is what matters for autonomy: an agent that succeeds 90% of the time per step fails a 10-step task more than 60% of the time, because errors compound multiplicatively. If you are going to let the agent run unsupervised, pass^k (consistency) is your true north, and it is brutal — far harsher than pass@k, and far more honest about whether you can trust the thing alone.

And the rule that separates signal from theater:

A change smaller than the confidence interval is noise. If your baseline is 80% ± 7% and your change measures 83%, you have learned nothing. The intervals overlap completely. Reporting “we improved 3 points” is, statistically, a lie. Either gather more samples (more cases × higher k) to shrink the interval, or accept that the change is in the noise floor and stop pretending otherwise.

The width of that interval is governed by your sample size — n cases times k runs. Small suites have wide intervals and can only detect large changes; that’s a real constraint to design around, not a bug. We’ll compute these intervals with a Wilson helper in the exercise. The discipline is non-negotiable: rates with intervals, never bare booleans, and changes within the interval are noise.

4. How evals run, operationally

The conceptual model is clean; the operational reality has a few hard parts worth naming, because this is where agent evals diverge most from running a unit suite.

Isolated, reproducible fixtures — one per trial. Each run must start from an identical, pristine world and not contaminate the next run. The pattern is dead simple and robust: keep a frozen fixture directory under version control; for each trial, copy it into a fresh temp dir and run the agent there. The agent edits the copy; you grade the copy; you delete the copy. Run 2 cannot see run 1’s mutations. (You’ll see exactly this — shutil.copytree into a tempfile.mkdtemp() — in the harness below.)

Mock the side-effecting tools — let the agent think it acted. If Scout’s real toolset sends emails, charges cards, opens PRs, or posts to Slack, you cannot fire those for real across thousands of eval runs. The technique: replace side-effecting tools with mocks that record the intended action and return a plausible success, so the agent proceeds through its loop as if the action happened. You grade the recorded intent (“did it attempt to email the right person with the right content?”) rather than a real send. This is the single biggest source of the mock-vs-reality gap (Real production issues, below) — the agent passed because your mock behaved more nicely than the real world does — so keep mocks faithful and treat a passing eval over mocks as necessary, not sufficient.

Parallelize — it’s embarrassingly parallel, with a cap. Cases and runs are independent: n × k trials with no data dependencies. Fan them out concurrently to turn an hour into a minute. The only constraint is a concurrency cap to respect API rate limits — a semaphore, nothing fancier.

Two tiers: smoke vs full — your fast-unit-vs-slow-integration split. You cannot afford to run the deep suite on every commit (agent suites are expensive — many model calls per run, times k, times n). So you tier it, exactly the way you already tier tests:

  • Smoke suite — small, cheap, k=1, a dozen core cases. Runs on every change as a fast gate. Catches gross breakage in seconds-to-minutes.
  • Full suite — the whole dataset, deep, k≥3 (or higher), with confidence intervals. Runs nightly and pre-release. This is the suite you gate ships on.

Record everything: score + full transcript + op metrics. A bare score is undebuggable. For every trial, persist the complete transcript (every message, tool call, and result — this is the trace Module 09 makes first-class), the score and grader rationale, and operational metrics: tokens, latency, step count, dollar cost. When a case fails, the transcript is the only thing that tells you why; when it passes, the op metrics tell you whether it passed efficiently (an agent that takes 40 steps and $2 to do a 4-step task is failing in a way no pass/fail captures).

5. Interpreting results — turning numbers into decisions

You have per-slice rates with intervals. Now what? Every comparison is relative. An absolute number (“76% pass”) is nearly meaningless in isolation — 76% might be triumphant or catastrophic depending on the task and your baseline. Evals earn their keep through three kinds of comparison:

  • Regression gating (vs. your own baseline, in CI). Store the current production system’s scores as the baseline. On every change, re-run the suite and block the merge if any slice drops beyond its confidence interval. This is the agent-world equivalent of a red build — and the thing that makes it safe to iterate on prompts and tools at all. Per-slice, not aggregate: a 4-point drop on bugfix masked by a 4-point rise on qa nets to zero overall and ships a real regression. Gate the slices.
  • Model selection (run the identical suite across candidates). When a new model ships, run your unchanged suite on old and new and read the per-slice diff. This is the payoff of the whole module: it converts a model upgrade from a terrifying leap of faith into a measured, one-day A/B test. You see precisely which slices improved and which regressed, and you decide with data. “The model changed” stops being existential and becomes a diff you can read.
  • Trend over time. Plot scores across runs. Slow drift — from prompt rot, a dependency bump, gradual fixture decay — is invisible run-to-run and obvious over weeks. The trend line catches the slow leak.

Validate the grader — the step everyone skips and then regrets. Your number is only as trustworthy as the thing producing it, and the grader can lie:

  • Calibrate the judge against humans (the discipline from §3b), and re-calibrate when you change the rubric or the judge model.
  • Spot-read passing transcripts, not just failing ones. Failing transcripts get read because failures demand attention. The insidious failure is passing-for-the-wrong-reason — the agent reached the right end state by luck, by exploiting a quirk of the fixture, or by a path that won’t generalize. Your grader sees green; reality will see red. The only defense is periodically reading transcripts that passed and asking “did it pass for a reason that will hold up?”

The decision loop, concretely. Run the suite, then:

  1. Within CI on every slice → ship. No slice dropped beyond its interval; the change is at worst neutral. Merge.
  2. A slice dropped beyond its interval → read the failing transcripts. Failures cluster — a dozen failures usually share one root cause (a prompt instruction the model now over-applies, a tool returning a new error shape, a context-window overflow on big fixtures). Find the cluster, fix the root cause, re-run.
  3. A new production failure appeared → mine it into a case. Freeze the situation as a regression case (the flywheel), confirm it now reproduces the failure, then fix it. The case is permanent insurance against re-breaking.

6. End-to-end vs component evals; and online evals

So far we’ve evaluated the whole agent loop end-to-end: give it a task, let it act, grade the final world. That’s the most important kind — it measures what the user experiences. But it has a weakness: when an end-to-end eval regresses, it tells you that something broke, not what. The failure could be in retrieval, in compaction, in the judge, in a tool.

Component evals localize the damage. You freeze the input to one component and test that component in isolation against a fixed expected behavior:

  • Compaction (Module 04): freeze a long transcript, run your summarizer, assert the summary preserves the load-bearing facts.
  • Retrieval (Module 05): freeze a query set with known-relevant docs, measure recall@k and precision — no agent loop at all.
  • The judge itself: freeze a set of human-labeled outputs and measure judge agreement. (Yes — you eval your evaluator. The judge is a component like any other.)

The pattern: end-to-end evals tell you the system regressed; component evals tell you which part. Run both. Component evals are cheaper, more deterministic, and they’re where a regression’s location becomes obvious.

Online / production evals — the bridge to Module 09. Everything above is offline: a fixed dataset, run in CI. But production is the real distribution, and it shifts. Online evals sample live traces and grade them continuously — run an LLM judge over (a sample of) real production runs, track the pass rate as a live metric, and alarm when it dips. This is the earliest possible warning that reality has diverged from your offline suite, and it’s where evals and observability merge. We pick it up properly in Module 09; the connection to hold now is that traces are the shared substrate — the same transcript you grade offline is the trace you watch online.

(a) Author the dataset (8–12 cases, mixed graders)

Each case is a directory: a fixture/ (the frozen world) and a case.yaml (task + tags + grader). Mix the grader families so you exercise the whole spectrum. A representative spread:

# evals/cases/bugfix_off_by_one/case.yaml
id: bugfix_off_by_one
slice: bugfix
difficulty: easy
task: "There is a failing test in this directory. Find and fix the bug so all tests pass. Do not edit the tests."
grader:
  type: programmatic
  check: pytest          # green test suite == pass. The gold standard.

# evals/cases/extract_invoice/case.yaml
id: extract_invoice
slice: extraction
difficulty: medium
task: "Read invoice.txt and write invoice.json containing keys: invoice_number, total_amount (number), due_date (YYYY-MM-DD)."
grader:
  type: assertions       # must / must_not predicates over the output file
  file: invoice.json
  must_be_valid_json: true
  must_contain: ["invoice_number", "total_amount", "due_date"]
  expect_json:
    invoice_number: "INV-4471"
    total_amount: 1840.00

# evals/cases/summarize_incident/case.yaml
id: summarize_incident
slice: summary
difficulty: hard
task: "Read incident.md and write a 3-sentence summary to summary.txt covering: the root cause, the customer impact, and the fix."
grader:
  type: judge            # genuinely fuzzy quality -> LLM-as-judge with a rubric
  file: summary.txt      # the agent's output, read from the mutated fixture copy
  source: incident.md    # the ground-truth source, read from the pristine fixture
  rubric: |
    Score 1-5. A 5 names the ROOT CAUSE (the database connection pool exhaustion),
    states the CUSTOMER IMPACT (checkout failures for ~12 minutes), and the FIX
    (raised pool size + added a circuit breaker). Deduct for hallucinated facts not
    in the source, for missing any of the three required elements, or for padding
    beyond 3 sentences. A 5 is concise, accurate, and complete.
  pass_threshold: 4

Round out to 8–12 with more bugfix cases (varying difficulty), a JSON-validity extraction, a knowledge-base QA case graded by assertions (must_contain the correct fact, must_not contain a known wrong answer), and at least one adversarial case (an empty file, a contradictory instruction) to probe the tail. The mix is deliberate: most cases programmatic/assertion (cheap, trustworthy), a few judge cases for the genuinely fuzzy ones.

(b) The graders 

# evals/graders.py
import json, subprocess
from pathlib import Path
from pydantic import BaseModel, Field
from anthropic import Anthropic

client = Anthropic()
JUDGE_MODEL = "claude-opus-4-8"   # strong judge, even though Scout runs on Sonnet

# --- 1. Programmatic: run the test suite in the (mutated) fixture copy --------
def grade_pytest(workdir: Path) -> tuple[bool, str]:
    r = subprocess.run(["pytest", "-q"], cwd=workdir, capture_output=True, text=True, timeout=120)
    return r.returncode == 0, r.stdout[-2000:] + r.stderr[-2000:]

# --- 2. Assertion list: must / must_not predicates over an output file --------
def grade_assertions(workdir: Path, spec: dict) -> tuple[bool, str]:
    target = workdir / spec["file"]
    if not target.exists():
        return False, f"expected output file {spec['file']} was never created"
    text = target.read_text()
    notes = []
    if spec.get("must_be_valid_json"):
        try:
            data = json.loads(text)
        except json.JSONDecodeError as e:
            return False, f"invalid JSON: {e}"
        for k, v in spec.get("expect_json", {}).items():
            if data.get(k) != v:
                notes.append(f"field {k}: expected {v!r}, got {data.get(k)!r}")
    for needle in spec.get("must_contain", []):
        if needle not in text:
            notes.append(f"missing required substring: {needle!r}")
    for needle in spec.get("must_not_contain", []):
        if needle in text:
            notes.append(f"contains forbidden substring: {needle!r}")
    return (len(notes) == 0), ("; ".join(notes) or "all assertions passed")

# --- 3. LLM-as-judge: structured verdict against a rubric ---------------------
class Verdict(BaseModel):
    evidence: list[str] = Field(description="Exact quotes from the output that justify the score. Cite BEFORE scoring.")
    reasoning: str = Field(description="How the evidence maps to the rubric.")
    score: int = Field(ge=1, le=5, description="1-5 per the rubric.")

JUDGE_SYSTEM = (
    "You are a strict, fair grader. Apply the rubric literally. "
    "First quote the exact spans of the OUTPUT that bear on the rubric, then reason, then score. "
    "Judge only against the rubric and the SOURCE — never reward length or style. "
    "If a required element is absent from the output, it is missing even if it is 'obvious'."
)

def grade_judge(workdir: Path, spec: dict, source_text: str) -> tuple[bool, str, int]:
    target = workdir / spec["file"]
    output = target.read_text() if target.exists() else "(no output file was produced)"
    resp = client.messages.parse(
        model=JUDGE_MODEL,
        max_tokens=1024,
        system=JUDGE_SYSTEM,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
            f"RUBRIC:\n{spec['rubric']}\n\nSOURCE:\n{source_text}\n\nOUTPUT TO GRADE:\n{output}"}],
        output_format=Verdict,
    )
    v: Verdict = resp.parsed_output
    passed = v.score >= spec["pass_threshold"]
    return passed, f"score={v.score} reason={v.reasoning} evidence={v.evidence}", v.score

Note the structured judge: evidence is required and ordered first so the model must cite before it scores; the verdict is a typed Verdict, never free prose; the system prompt explicitly names and disarms the length and style biases.

(c) The runner — fresh fixture per trial, k runs, parallel, full recording 

# evals/harness.py
import shutil, tempfile, json, yaml, time
from pathlib import Path
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor
from scout.loop import run_agent          # adapt to your Scout entrypoint
from graders import grade_pytest, grade_assertions, grade_judge

CASES = Path(__file__).parent / "cases"
RUNS  = Path(__file__).parent / "runs"
K = 3                # runs per case (full suite). Smoke suite sets K=1.
MAX_CONCURRENCY = 8  # respect API rate limits

def load_cases(slice_filter=None):
    cases = []
    for d in sorted(CASES.iterdir()):
        if not (d / "case.yaml").exists():
            continue
        spec = yaml.safe_load((d / "case.yaml").read_text())
        if slice_filter and spec["slice"] != slice_filter:
            continue
        cases.append((d, spec))
    return cases

def run_trial(case_dir: Path, spec: dict, trial_idx: int) -> dict:
    # Isolated, reproducible: copy the frozen fixture into a fresh temp dir.
    work = Path(tempfile.mkdtemp(prefix=f"{spec['id']}_{trial_idx}_"))
    shutil.copytree(case_dir / "fixture", work, dirs_exist_ok=True)
    t0 = time.time()
    transcript = run_agent(task=spec["task"], cwd=str(work))   # Scout acts on the COPY
    latency = time.time() - t0

    g = spec["grader"]; score_num = None
    if g["type"] == "programmatic" and g["check"] == "pytest":
        passed, detail = grade_pytest(work)
    elif g["type"] == "assertions":
        passed, detail = grade_assertions(work, g)
    elif g["type"] == "judge":
        # Read the source the judge grades against from the PRISTINE fixture
        # (the case names the source file under grader.source).
        source = (case_dir / "fixture" / g["source"]).read_text()
        passed, detail, score_num = grade_judge(work, g, source)
    else:
        raise ValueError(f"unknown grader {g['type']}")

    rec = {
        "case": spec["id"], "slice": spec["slice"],
        "trial": trial_idx, "passed": bool(passed), "score": score_num,
        "detail": detail, "latency_s": round(latency, 2),
        "tokens": getattr(transcript, "usage", None) and {
            "in": transcript.usage.input_tokens, "out": transcript.usage.output_tokens},
        "steps": sum(1 for m in transcript.messages if m["role"] == "assistant"),
    }
    # Record the FULL transcript (the trace) + score + op metrics.
    out = RUNS / spec["id"] / f"trial_{trial_idx}"
    out.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
    (out / "record.json").write_text(json.dumps(rec, indent=2, default=str))
    (out / "transcript.json").write_text(json.dumps(transcript.messages, indent=2, default=str))
    shutil.rmtree(work, ignore_errors=True)   # clean up the copy
    return rec

def run_suite(slice_filter=None, k=K) -> list[dict]:
    jobs = [(d, spec, i) for (d, spec) in load_cases(slice_filter) for i in range(k)]
    with ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=MAX_CONCURRENCY) as ex:
        return list(ex.map(lambda a: run_trial(*a), jobs))

(d) Aggregate with confidence intervals 

# evals/stats.py
import math
from collections import defaultdict

def wilson(successes: int, n: int, z: float = 1.96) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Wilson score interval for a binomial proportion. Returns (rate, low, high)."""
    if n == 0:
        return 0.0, 0.0, 0.0
    p = successes / n
    denom = 1 + z**2 / n
    center = (p + z**2 / (2 * n)) / denom
    half = (z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z**2 / (4 * n**2))) / denom
    return p, max(0.0, center - half), min(1.0, center + half)

def aggregate(records: list[dict]) -> dict:
    # pass rate per slice, with Wilson CI — the headline numbers.
    by_slice = defaultdict(lambda: [0, 0])     # slice -> [successes, n]
    by_case  = defaultdict(list)               # case  -> [bool, ...]  (for pass^k / pass@k)
    for r in records:
        s = by_slice[r["slice"]]; s[1] += 1; s[0] += int(r["passed"])
        by_case[r["case"]].append(r["passed"])
    report = {"slices": {}, "reliability": {}}
    for sl, (succ, n) in by_slice.items():
        rate, lo, hi = wilson(succ, n)
        report["slices"][sl] = {"rate": round(rate, 3), "ci": [round(lo, 3), round(hi, 3)], "n": n}
    for case, results in by_case.items():
        report["reliability"][case] = {
            "pass_at_k": any(results),          # >=1 success: salvageable-with-retry
            "pass_pow_k": all(results),         # ALL succeed: the autonomy metric
            "consistency": round(sum(results) / len(results), 2),
        }
    return report

Print it and you get exactly the artifact §3c demanded — per-slice rates with intervals, plus per-case pass^k:

slice=bugfix      rate=0.83  ci=[0.59, 0.94]  n=18      <- wide interval: only 18 trials
slice=extraction  rate=0.94  ci=[0.73, 0.99]  n=12
slice=summary     rate=0.67  ci=[0.39, 0.86]  n=9
reliability bugfix_off_by_one  pass^k=True   consistency=1.00
reliability summarize_incident pass^k=False  consistency=0.67   <- inconsistent: autonomy risk

(e) Use it for real — a ship/no-ship decision

Now do the thing the whole harness exists for. Record the current runs/ as your baseline. Then make a real change to Scout’s system prompt — say, add “Be extremely concise; prefer the shortest correct answer.” Re-run the full suite and compare per slice:

base = aggregate(load_records("runs_baseline"))
cand = aggregate(run_suite())
for sl in base["slices"]:
    b, c = base["slices"][sl], cand["slices"][sl]
    # Regression rule: candidate's rate dropped BELOW the baseline's lower CI bound.
    regressed = c["rate"] < b["ci"][0]
    print(f"{sl:12} base={b['rate']:.2f}{b['ci']}  cand={c['rate']:.2f}{c['ci']}  "
          f"{'❌ REGRESSION' if regressed else 'ok'}")

Make the call. If no slice dropped below its baseline lower bound, ship — the change is at worst neutral. If summary rose (concise summaries score better on the rubric) but bugfix dropped beyond its interval (the agent got too terse and stopped explaining its reasoning, hurting multi-step debugging) — no-ship, and you’ve learned something real about that prompt change that you’d never have seen in a demo. This is the entire point: a prompt edit is no longer a vibe, it’s a measured per-slice diff.

(f) Calibrate the judge

Before you trust the summary slice, prove the judge isn’t lying. Hand-grade 5 summary outputs yourself (1–5), then have the judge grade the same 5, and measure agreement:

human  = {"t0": 5, "t1": 3, "t2": 4, "t3": 2, "t4": 4}     # your labels
judge  = {t: grade_judge(...)[2] for t in human}            # judge's scores
exact  = sum(human[t] == judge[t] for t in human) / len(human)
within1 = sum(abs(human[t] - judge[t]) <= 1 for t in human) / len(human)
print(f"exact agreement={exact:.0%}  within-1={within1:.0%}")

If within-1 is high (≈100%) and exact agreement is decent, the judge is usable — trust the summary slice. If they diverge, your judge is the problem, not Scout: tighten the rubric (more concrete anchors per score), add the disarm-the-bias instructions, or switch judge models, and re-calibrate. Never trust a judge slice you haven’t calibrated.

(g) Mine a failure into a regression case

Find a case Scout fails (or induce one — feed it a gnarly real task it bungles). Freeze that exact situation: snapshot the input files into a new cases/<name>/fixture/, write a case.yaml whose grading key encodes the correct outcome, and confirm the case currently fails. It is now a permanent regression test — the flywheel in miniature. Wire run_suite(slice_filter="smoke") with k=1 into your CI on every push, and the full suite into a nightly job. You now have a reusable eval harness with a smoke gate, a full suite, calibrated judging, and a flywheel. That harness is worth more than any single prompt improvement, because it makes every future improvement measurable.

Real production issues

  • Flaky, noisy evals from too-low k or an uncalibrated judge. Run k=1 and treat the boolean as truth and you’ll “improve” things that were noise and “regress” things that weren’t. The two fixes: raise k until the CI is tight enough to detect the effect you care about, and calibrate the judge so its scores mean something. Most “our evals are useless” complaints are one of these two, untreated.
  • Goodhart / overfitting to your own suite. Iterate against a fixed eval long enough and you tune the agent to the eval, not the task. Defenses: keep growing the suite from production (the flywheel — a moving target can’t be overfit), and hold out a slice of cases the agent is never tuned against, checked only at release. If held-out scores lag the tuned set, you’re overfitting.
  • The mock-vs-reality gap — passed the eval, failed in prod. Your mocked send_email returned a clean success; the real SMTP server rejected the address. Mocks that behave more nicely than reality manufacture false confidence. Keep mocks faithful to real failure modes, and treat a green offline suite as necessary, not sufficient — back it with online evals on real traces (Module 09).
  • Passing-for-the-wrong-reason. The agent reached the right end state by exploiting a fixture quirk, or by luck that won’t generalize. The grader sees green; production sees red. Only defense: periodically read passing transcripts, not just failing ones, and ask “would this path hold on a slightly different input?”
  • Judge biases lying with confidence. Length, self-style, and position biases make a judge confidently wrong — and confident wrongness is the worst kind, because it survives review. Disarm in the rubric, force evidence citation before scoring, swap order in pairwise comparisons, and re-calibrate against humans whenever you touch the judge.
  • Coverage gaps in the long tail. A finite suite samples the task distribution; agents fail in the unsampled tail you never wrote a case for. You can’t test your way to full coverage. Online evals on real traffic are how the tail surfaces — and each tail failure becomes a mined case, narrowing the gap over time.
  • Cost of big agent suites. A full agentic suite is many model calls × k × n — minutes and real dollars per run. That’s exactly why you tier: cheap smoke gate on every change, expensive full suite nightly/pre-release. Don’t run the deep suite on every commit; don’t ship on the smoke suite alone.
  • Model upgrades silently breaking prompts (prompt rot). The provider improves the model; your prompt, tuned to the old one, regresses on a slice or two. With no eval suite this is invisible until a user reports it. With one, the upgrade is a one-day measured A/B — you read the per-slice diff and decide. This is the single most important thing evals buy you: they turn “the model changed” from existential risk into a diff you can read.
  • The temptation to skip evals because they’re unglamorous. No one demos an eval harness. It ships no user-facing feature. So under deadline it’s the first thing cut — and the teams that cut it ship agents that fail silently and degrade invisibly, because they have no instrument pointed at quality. The teams that ship reliable agents treat eval engineering as first-class, resourced comparably to product work, owned not delegated. Eval development is roughly as much effort as product development. That sounds expensive until you price the alternative: shipping a stochastic system into production with no way to know if it works. The eval suite is the product’s quality, made measurable. Build it first-class or build it never.

  • Anthropic Cookbook — evaluations & LLM-as-judge recipes: end-to-end judge construction, rubric design, and calibration in code.
  • “Judging LLM-as-a-Judge” (Zheng et al., MT-Bench / Chatbot Arena): the canonical study of judge biases (length, position, self-enhancement) and how to measure judge–human agreement.

Learn the from-scratch harness first — the platform is a convenience over the concepts you just built, not a substitute for understanding them.
