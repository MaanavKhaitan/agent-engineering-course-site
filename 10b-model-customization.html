Module 10B — Model Customization: Fine-Tuning, RFT & When to Train

Modules 1–10 taught you to push a frozen, general-purpose model as far as it goes: prompting, context, RAG, tools, evals, and the reliability/cost/performance levers. This module is about the lever you reach for last — changing the weights themselves.

Here is the dominant message, stated up front so you don’t lose it in the details: for most teams, the right answer is “not yet.” Fine-tuning is the highest-cost, highest-ops, least-flexible lever in your toolbox. It freezes behavior into a frozen artifact at the exact moment when base models are leaping forward every few months. The teams that win the customization game are almost never the ones who fine-tuned early — they’re the ones who built evals and data flywheels (which appreciate) and reached for training only when a real capability or cost ceiling forced their hand, with the data and evals in hand to prove it worked.

This is an engineering-economics module, not an ML-internals module. We will not teach you backprop or LoRA rank selection. We will teach you the decision framework: which gap each lever closes, when training is the wrong tool entirely (it usually is), and the one case — distillation — where the ROI is genuinely clean.

Learning objectives

By the end you will be able to:

  • Place any capability gap on the prompting → few-shot → context/RAG → tools → fine-tuning ladder and justify the cheapest lever that closes it.
  • Explain precisely what supervised fine-tuning (SFT) does and does not do — and why “fine-tune the model on our docs” is the classic wrong-tool error.
  • Distinguish SFT, preference tuning (DPO/RLHF), reinforcement fine-tuning (RFT) with verifiable rewards, LoRA/PEFT, and distillation at an engineer’s level of detail.
  • Connect RFT directly to your Module 08 eval grader (the grader is the reward signal).
  • Make the honest call on when to fine-tune vs. when not to — and recognize the data-quality and “frozen asset” traps that silently sink fine-tuning projects.
  • Run a small distillation demo: label with a strong model, serve a cheap one, measure the delta with your eval harness.

1. The capability/cost ladder

Every customization decision starts here. When the model isn’t doing what you need, walk down this ladder and stop at the first rung that closes the gap. Each rung is cheaper, faster to iterate, and more flexible than the one below it.

Lever Closes the gap when… Iteration speed Ops cost Flexibility
Prompting The model can do it but isn’t, given the current instructions Seconds (edit a string) ~0 Total — change anytime
Few-shot examples The model needs to see the format/pattern, not be told Seconds ~0 Total
Context / RAG The model lacks knowledge — facts, docs, current data Minutes (index docs) Low–medium High — update the index, not the model
Tools The model needs to act or fetch live state (APIs, code, search) Hours (build a tool) Medium High — add/remove tools
Fine-tuning A real gap remains after all of the above and you have data + evals Days–weeks per cycle High (training + serving + retraining treadmill) Low — behavior is frozen into weights

The asymmetry is the whole point. A prompt edit is a one-line diff you can ship and revert in the same afternoon. A fine-tune is a multi-day pipeline, a new serving artifact, a new thing to monitor, and a behavior you’ve frozen into weights that go stale the moment the base model improves. You should feel real resistance dropping to the bottom rung.

A rough decision tree:

Is the model missing KNOWLEDGE (facts, docs, fresh data)?
  → RAG / context.  Fine-tuning does NOT add knowledge. (See §2.)

Does it need to ACT or read live state?
  → Tools.

Does it know what to do but produce the wrong FORMAT / STYLE / TONE?
  → Try prompting + few-shot first.
  → If you need it bit-exact every time → structured outputs, or SFT as a last resort.

Is it correct but too SLOW / EXPENSIVE at high volume on a NARROW task?
  → Distillation (the one clean fine-tuning win). (See §6.)

None of the above, real gap remains, you have data + evals?
  → Now consider fine-tuning.

Note that “the model is too expensive” usually has a cheaper answer than training: prompt caching, model tiering (route easy calls to a cheaper model), and batching capture most of the cost win without any custom weights (Module 10). Exhaust those first.

✅ Rule of thumb: if you can close the gap with a string you can edit in production, do that. Reserve weight changes for gaps that survive every cheaper lever.

2. What fine-tuning is — and what it isn’t

This is the single most expensive misconception in the field, so we lead with it:

Supervised fine-tuning teaches FORMAT, STYLE, TONE, and narrow task BEHAVIOR. It does NOT reliably inject new FACTS or KNOWLEDGE, and it won’t substantially improve reasoning.

SFT continues training a base model on a dataset of input→output examples. Gradient descent nudges the weights so the model’s outputs look more like your examples. What that reliably changes:

  • Format — “always emit our exact ticket JSON,” “always answer in this rubric structure.”
  • Style and tone — terse vs. expansive, our brand voice, a specific persona.
  • Narrow task behavior — a classification boundary, an extraction pattern, a consistent way of handling a specific kind of request.

What it does not reliably do:

  • Add knowledge. Training on your internal wiki does not make the model know your wiki. It will absorb the surface form and may parrot a few facts, but it will also confidently hallucinate, can’t be updated when a doc changes without retraining, and gives you no citations. Knowledge is RAG’s job (Module 05). If the gap is “the model doesn’t know X,” fine-tuning is the wrong tool, full stop. This is the error to internalize: the request “fine-tune the model on our docs” is almost always a RAG request in disguise.
  • Make the model fundamentally smarter. SFT teaches the model to imitate outputs; it does not teach it to reason better than its base capability. If the base model can’t solve the problem, examples of solved problems mostly teach it to imitate the shape of a solution, not to actually reason to one.

The clean mental model: RAG and tools change what the model can SEE; fine-tuning changes how the model BEHAVES with what it already sees. Confuse the two and you’ll spend weeks training a model to do something a retrieval index does better, cheaper, and updatably.

📖 The corollary: fine-tuning works best on tasks that are narrow and stable in behavior but high in volume — exactly the profile where you’d also want the cheaper, faster model. That’s why distillation (§6) is the sweet spot.

3. The methods (engineer’s-eye view)

You don’t need the math, but you should know what each technique buys you. Each of these is one tight paragraph.

SFT (supervised fine-tuning). The baseline. Train on (input, ideal_output) pairs; the model learns to produce outputs like your examples. Best for format/style/behavior on a narrow task. This is what “fine-tuning” means by default and what most provider fine-tuning APIs expose.

Preference tuning (DPO / RLHF). Instead of one ideal output per input, you provide pairs — a preferred and a rejected response — and the model learns to favor the preferred style. RLHF does this via a learned reward model and reinforcement learning; DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) skips the separate reward model and optimizes preferences directly, which is simpler and cheaper to run. Use this to align to subjective preferences (“we like answers that hedge less,” “we prefer this tone”) where there’s no single correct output but there is a clear better/worse.

RFT / reinforcement fine-tuning with verifiable rewards. The most agent-relevant method. Instead of labeled outputs, you fine-tune against a reward signal: the model generates attempts, a grader scores each one, and the weights are updated toward higher-scoring behavior. The grader can be anything programmatic — did the unit test pass? did the extracted JSON validate against the schema? did the eval mark it correct? This is powerful precisely when you can check an answer more easily than you can write the ideal one. The direct tie to this course: your Module 08 eval grader can BE the reward. If you already have a programmatic grader that returns a score, you already have the hardest piece of an RFT pipeline. (More in §7’s exercise.)

LoRA / PEFT (parameter-efficient fine-tuning). Full fine-tuning updates every weight — expensive to train and to serve (a whole new model copy). LoRA and other PEFT methods train a small set of adapter weights on top of a frozen base, capturing most of the benefit at a fraction of the training cost and storage. Practically: cheaper to train, faster to iterate, and many adapters can share one base model in serving. For most teams doing SFT, PEFT is the default; full fine-tuning is reserved for cases where adapters genuinely underperform.

Distillation. Train a small, cheap model to imitate a large, expensive one on a narrow task. You generate high-quality labels with a frontier model (here: claude-opus-4-8 or claude-fable-5), then fine-tune a small model (or few-shot a cheap one like claude-haiku-4-5) on those labels. The payoff is the cleanest in this whole module: near-frontier quality at a fraction of the cost and latency, on a task narrow enough that the small model can actually cover it. This is the one fine-tuning play that routinely pays for itself, and it’s the focus of the exercise.

4. The agent connection & the frontier

Here’s why this module sits before the security module and not in a vacuum: customization is increasingly about the boundary between your scaffolding and the model’s weights — and that boundary is moving.

Frontier models are now routinely RL-trained against their own harnesses — the exact tool-call formats, retrieval patterns, and interaction loops they’re expected to use in production. Research like Search-R1 (training a model to interleave reasoning and search calls) is the visible edge of a broad trend: capability that used to live in your scaffolding is migrating into the weights. A model RL-trained to call search well doesn’t need your elaborate retrieval-orchestration prompt. A model trained against a coding harness doesn’t need your hand-tuned “here’s how to use the edit tool” instructions.

The implication, which connects directly to Module 13 (the frontier): every component of your scaffolding encodes an assumption about what the model can’t do. Your clever multi-step prompt assumes the model can’t plan. Your retrieval reranker assumes it can’t filter results itself. Your output-repair logic assumes it can’t follow the format. Each new model generation deletes some of those assumptions — and with them, the scaffolding you built around them.

This reframes the whole customization decision as an appreciation vs. depreciation question:

  • Appreciating assets: your evals, your labeled data, your tool/integration surface, your guardrails. These survive — and get more valuable — across model generations. A good eval suite tells you instantly whether the new model is better. A clean dataset can re-train or re-distill onto the new base. Tools and integrations are model-agnostic.
  • Depreciating assets: prompt-engineering tricks and frozen fine-tunes. A scaffolding hack that squeezes 5 points out of this generation’s model may be irrelevant — or actively harmful — on the next. A fine-tune frozen against today’s base model goes stale the moment a better base ships.

So when you’re deciding whether to fine-tune, ask: am I building an appreciating asset or a depreciating one? Pour effort into evals, data, and integrations. Treat both scaffolding tricks and frozen fine-tunes as things you expect to throw away when the base model leaps.

5. The data problem

Fine-tuning runs on a dataset of good examples, and this is where most projects quietly fail. Three sources:

  1. Production traces — the data flywheel (Modules 08/09). Your logged agent runs, filtered to the good ones (and ideally graded by your eval harness), are the highest-signal training data you have: real inputs, real distribution, real edge cases. This is the asset that compounds — every day in production grows it. If you have good tracing and eval infrastructure, you’re already accumulating the dataset.
  2. Human labeling. Experts write or correct ideal outputs. Highest quality per example, most expensive per example. Use it to seed or to fix the failure modes your traces don’t cover.
  3. Distillation from a bigger model. Generate labels with a frontier model (§6). Cheap, fast, and scalable — the quality ceiling is the frontier model’s quality on the task.

The non-negotiable rule: quality beats quantity, by a lot. Dozens-to-hundreds of excellent examples routinely outperform thousands of mediocre ones. And the failure is silent: a fine-tune trained on inconsistent, mislabeled, or off-distribution data doesn’t error — it trains perfectly well and produces a model that’s confidently worse, in ways you won’t see unless your evals catch them. Garbage in, confidently-wrong out. This is why evals are a prerequisite, not a nice-to-have: without them you cannot tell whether your data was good or whether your fine-tune helped.

6. The honest decision — when to fine-tune (and when not to)

This is the crux of the module. Be ruthless here.

✅ DO consider fine-tuning when ALL of these hold

  • You’ve genuinely maxed out prompting + context + tools and a real, measured gap remains.
  • You need lower latency or cost at scale — most cleanly, distilling a frontier model into a small one for a narrow, high-volume task (e.g. millions of classification calls/day).
  • You need very consistent format/style/behavior that prompting can’t reliably enforce.
  • You have, or can generate, good training data (production traces, human labels, or distillation).
  • You have evals that can PROVE it helped. Non-negotiable.

🛑 DON’T fine-tune when ANY of these hold

  • You need new or changing knowledge → use RAG (Module 05). Fine-tuning can’t add it and can’t keep it current.
  • The task is broad or the spec is still moving → fine-tuning freezes behavior; you’ll be freezing the wrong thing and retraining constantly.
  • You lack eval infrastructure → you literally cannot tell whether it worked. Build evals first; you may find you don’t need to fine-tune at all.
  • A frontier model already does it with prompting → you’d be trading a flexible, improving asset for a frozen, depreciating one.
  • You’re early-stage → your spec, your data, and your understanding of the problem are all still changing. Freezing any of them is premature.

For the large majority of teams, the honest read on these lists is “not yet.” That is the correct, responsible answer, and saying it out loud has saved more engineering-quarters than any fine-tuning success story.

7. Economics & ops reality

Even when fine-tuning is justified, price in the full lifecycle — not just the training run:

  • Upfront training cost — compute for the run, plus the (larger) cost of building and cleaning the dataset.
  • The retraining treadmill. Your data drifts (new product, new edge cases) and the base model improves. Both force re-training. A fine-tune is not a one-time cost; it’s a standing maintenance commitment.
  • A frozen asset that goes stale. The fine-tuned model is pinned to whatever base it was trained on. When a markedly better base model ships, your fine-tune doesn’t automatically inherit the gains — and your scaffolding around it rots (Module 13). You’re often choosing between “stay on a now-worse fine-tuned model” and “redo the whole pipeline on the new base.”
  • Provider lock-in. Your fine-tune lives on the platform you trained it on. It’s not portable; switching providers means re-training.
  • Contrast with evals + data — the appreciating asset. Every dollar into your eval suite and labeled dataset retains value across model generations and providers. Every dollar into a specific frozen fine-tune is pinned to one base model on one platform.

Two practical conclusions:

  1. Distillation is the cleanest ROI because it targets exactly the case where the economics work: a stable, narrow, high-volume task where shaving cost and latency compounds across millions of calls, and where re-distilling onto a new base is cheap (you regenerate labels and retrain the small model).
  2. The cost levers from Module 10 usually capture most of the win without any fine-tuning. Prompt caching, model tiering, and batching can cut your bill substantially with zero custom weights, zero retraining treadmill, and zero lock-in. Try them first; reach for distillation only when you’ve measured that they’re not enough.

🛠️ Exercise

This exercise is deliberately lighter than other modules’ — customization is infrastructure-heavy, and the real learning here is judgment, not pipeline-plumbing. Do part (a) carefully (it’s the whole point), run the small demo in (b), and sketch (c).

(a) Decision drill — the core of this exercise

For each problem, decide: prompt / few-shot / RAG / tools / fine-tune (and if fine-tune, which method). Write one sentence of justification before reading the answer.

  1. “Our agent must answer questions from our internal wiki, which is updated daily.”
  2. “Our agent must output our exact ticket-JSON schema, every single time, with no drift.”
  3. “We run 5M cheap text-classification calls per day and want frontier-model quality at Haiku cost.”
Answers 1. **RAG (Module 05) — not fine-tuning.** The gap is *knowledge*, and it *changes daily*. Fine-tuning can't add it and can't keep it current; you'd be retraining every day and still hallucinating. Index the wiki, retrieve, and cite. This is the canonical wrong-tool trap. 2. **Structured outputs first; SFT only if that's not enough.** The gap is *format consistency*. Try the cheapest lever — structured-output / JSON-schema enforcement (Module 04/08) — which guarantees the shape without touching weights. If you genuinely need behavior beyond what schema enforcement covers (e.g. consistent *content* choices within the schema), SFT on a few hundred clean examples is the next rung. Note: format/style is exactly what SFT is *good* at. 3. **Distillation.** This is the textbook case: narrow task, enormous volume, cost/latency-sensitive. Generate high-quality labels with `claude-opus-4-8`, then either few-shot `claude-haiku-4-5` or fine-tune a small model on those labels. Verify with your eval harness that the cheap model is close enough, and the cost delta pays for the whole project many times over. (First confirm prompt caching + tiering alone don't already get you there.) The three answers trace the ladder: **knowledge → RAG, format → structured-output/SFT, cost-at-scale → distillation.**

(b) Distillation mini-demo

Pick one narrow task (sentiment of support messages, intent classification, short-text categorization — anything with a checkable answer). Then:

Step 1 — Generate ~50 high-quality labels with a strong model.

import anthropic

client = anthropic.Anthropic()
STRONG_MODEL = "claude-opus-4-8"   # or "claude-fable-5" for the hardest tasks

# Your raw, unlabeled inputs for the narrow task.
inputs = [
    "The new dashboard is gorgeous but it logs me out every 10 minutes.",
    "Cancel my subscription. This is the third time I've asked.",
    "Works fine, no complaints.",
    # ... ~50 total
]

LABELS = ["positive", "negative", "neutral", "mixed"]

def strong_label(text: str) -> str:
    resp = client.messages.create(
        model=STRONG_MODEL,
        max_tokens=16000,
        thinking={"type": "adaptive"},
        output_config={
            "format": {
                "type": "json_schema",
                "schema": {
                    "type": "object",
                    "properties": {"label": {"type": "string", "enum": LABELS}},
                    "required": ["label"],
                    "additionalProperties": False,
                },
            }
        },
        messages=[{"role": "user",
                   "content": f"Classify the sentiment of this support message.\n\n{text}"}],
    )
    import json
    text_block = next(b.text for b in resp.content if b.type == "text")
    return json.loads(text_block)["label"]

# This labeled set is your distillation dataset (and a held-out eval slice).
dataset = [{"text": t, "label": strong_label(t)} for t in inputs]

Step 2 — Approximate it cheaply. Two options:

Option A — few-shot a cheap model (no training infra needed): pick your 5–8 clearest labeled examples as few-shot exemplars and run claude-haiku-4-5 against them.

CHEAP_MODEL = "claude-haiku-4-5"

few_shot = dataset[:6]   # use your cleanest, most representative examples
exemplars = "\n".join(f"Message: {d['text']}\nLabel: {d['label']}" for d in few_shot)

def cheap_label(text: str) -> str:
    resp = client.messages.create(
        model=CHEAP_MODEL,
        max_tokens=256,
        messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
            f"Classify sentiment as one of {LABELS}.\n\n{exemplars}\n\nMessage: {text}\nLabel:"}],
    )
    out = next(b.text for b in resp.content if b.type == "text").strip().lower()
    return next((l for l in LABELS if l in out), "neutral")

Option B — fine-tune a small model (if you have access). Using a provider with a public fine-tuning API — OpenAI is the concrete example here — the flow is conceptually: write your dataset to JSONL as (prompt, completion) / chat pairs, upload it, and kick off a job:

# Provider-honest note: OpenAI exposes a public fine-tuning API (and reinforcement
# fine-tuning). Anthropic's general guidance leans toward prompting + context + tools
# over fine-tuning for most cases; some fine-tuning is available via cloud platforms —
# check provider docs rather than assuming a first-party endpoint.

from openai import OpenAI
oai = OpenAI()

# 1. Upload your distillation dataset as JSONL (chat-format examples).
training_file = oai.files.create(
    file=open("distill_dataset.jsonl", "rb"),
    purpose="fine-tune",
)

# 2. Launch the fine-tuning job on a small base model.
job = oai.fine_tuning.jobs.create(
    training_file=training_file.id,
    model="gpt-4.1-mini",   # a small/cheap base — the distillation target
)
# 3. Poll job status; when it succeeds, you get a fine-tuned model ID to serve.

Step 3 — Measure with your Module 08 eval harness. Hold out a labeled slice the cheap model never saw, and compare cheap-vs-strong on both quality and cost/latency:

holdout = dataset[40:]   # never used as few-shot / training

def accuracy(predict_fn):
    correct = sum(predict_fn(d["text"]) == d["label"] for d in holdout)
    return correct / len(holdout)

print("Cheap model accuracy vs. strong-model labels:", accuracy(cheap_label))

# Cost delta (per the Module 10 pricing levers):
#   claude-opus-4-8 : $5.00 in / $25.00 out per 1M tokens
#   claude-haiku-4-5: $1.00 in /  $5.00 out per 1M tokens
# → ~5x cheaper per token AND lower latency. Across 5M calls/day, that delta is the
#   entire business case. The eval number tells you whether the quality is acceptable.

The learning: distillation is only worth it if the eval says the cheap model is close enough and the cost/latency delta is large enough. Both numbers come from your existing harness — that’s why evals are the prerequisite.

(c) Connect RFT to your eval grader

Sketch (no need to run) how the programmatic grader you built in Module 08 becomes a verifiable reward:

# Module 08 gave you something shaped like this — a grader that returns a score.
def grade(model_output, example) -> float:
    # e.g. did the JSON validate? did the unit test pass? exact-match the gold label?
    return 1.0 if is_correct(model_output, example) else 0.0

# In an RFT loop, that grader IS the reward signal:
#   1. Sample N candidate outputs from the model for each training input.
#   2. Score each with grade(...)  ← your eval grader, unchanged.
#   3. Update weights toward higher-scoring outputs.
# No labeled "ideal output" required — only the ability to CHECK an output.

The insight worth keeping: anywhere you can programmatically check an answer more easily than write the ideal one, you have a candidate reward function. Your eval harness isn’t just how you measure customization — it can be the thing you train against.

Real production issues

  • Fine-tuning to add knowledge (the #1 wrong tool). A team fine-tunes on their docs to make the agent “know” the product, ships it, and gets confident hallucinations with no citations and no way to update when a doc changes. The fix is always RAG (Module 05). Watch for this disguised as “train it on our data.”
  • Frozen fine-tunes going stale. You fine-tuned on last year’s base model. This year’s base model, used with plain prompting, beats your fine-tune outright — but you’re pinned to the old base. Now you’re on the retraining treadmill just to keep pace, or stuck shipping a model that’s worse than the default.
  • Provider lock-in. Your fine-tune lives on one platform. Switching providers — for cost, capability, or compliance — means re-doing the whole pipeline. The dataset is portable; the fine-tune is not.
  • The data-quality trap (silent). Inconsistent or mislabeled training data produces a confidently-worse model with no error and no warning. You only catch it if your evals are good enough to see the regression. Many teams don’t, and ship a quiet degradation.
  • Fine-tuning without evals. With no eval harness you cannot answer “did this help?” — so you can’t justify the cost, can’t catch regressions, and can’t tell good data from bad. This is the most common reason a fine-tuning project produces no defensible value.
  • Premature fine-tuning (freezing the wrong behavior). An early-stage team fine-tunes before the spec stabilizes, freezes a behavior that turns out to be wrong two weeks later, and spends the next month retraining. Frozen behavior + moving target = thrash.
  • Underestimating ops cost. Teams budget the training run and forget the dataset curation, the serving artifact, the monitoring, the retraining cadence, and the lock-in. The training run is often the cheapest line item.
  • The strategic miss: optimizing the depreciating asset. Effort poured into scaffolding tricks and frozen fine-tunes depreciates as models improve; effort poured into evals, data, integrations, and guardrails appreciates. The teams that win invest in the latter and reach for fine-tuning only at scale, with data and evals in hand — most often for distillation. For most teams, the right posture is exactly: prompt + context + tools + a frontier model, and revisit fine-tuning only when scale forces it.
